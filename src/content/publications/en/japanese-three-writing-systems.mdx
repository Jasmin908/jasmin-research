Most languages use one writing system. Japanese uses three at the same time, often within a single sentence: **Hiragana**, **Katakana** and **Kanji**. This sounds complicated, but each system has a clear job. Once you know what each one does, Japanese text starts to make sense. This guide explains all three, plus Romaji and the two ways of reading Kanji.

## An overview

| System | Example | What it is | Main use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hiragana | <Ja>ひらがな</Ja> | 46 basic sound characters, rounded shapes | Grammar endings, particles, native words |
| Katakana | <Ja>カタカナ</Ja> | 46 basic sound characters, angular shapes | Loanwords, foreign names, emphasis |
| Kanji | <Ja>漢字</Ja> | Characters adopted from Chinese, each with a meaning | Most nouns and the stems of verbs and adjectives |

Hiragana and Katakana together are called **kana**. Both are *syllabaries*: each character stands for a syllable such as *ka* or *mi*, not a single letter. Both were derived from parts or simplified forms of Chinese characters [@britannica-kana].

## Hiragana

**Hiragana** is usually the first script learners study. It has **46 basic characters** and covers every sound in Japanese. Children's books are often written mostly in Hiragana, and in normal texts it's used for:

- **Particles**, small words that mark the role of a word in the sentence, such as <Ja>は</Ja> (*wa*), <Ja>を</Ja> (*o*) and <Ja>に</Ja> (*ni*).
- **Grammatical endings** of verbs and adjectives, called *okurigana*: in <Ja>飲みます</Ja> (*nomimasu*, "drink"), <Ja>みます</Ja> is Hiragana.
- **Words without common Kanji**, or when the Kanji is rare.

Two small marks create more sounds from the basic characters:

- The **dakuten** ( <Ja>゛</Ja> ) makes a consonant voiced: <Ja>か</Ja> *ka* → <Ja>が</Ja> *ga*, <Ja>さ</Ja> *sa* → <Ja>ざ</Ja> *za*, <Ja>た</Ja> *ta* → <Ja>だ</Ja> *da*, <Ja>は</Ja> *ha* → <Ja>ば</Ja> *ba*.
- The **handakuten** ( <Ja>゜</Ja> ) turns *h* into *p*: <Ja>は</Ja> *ha* → <Ja>ぱ</Ja> *pa*.

A small <Ja>っ</Ja> doubles the following consonant, as in <Ja>きって</Ja> (*kitte*, "stamp").

## Katakana

**Katakana** has the same **46 basic sounds** as Hiragana, but with more angular shapes. It's mainly used for:

- **Loanwords** from other languages: <Ja>コーヒー</Ja> (*kōhī*, coffee), <Ja>テレビ</Ja> (*terebi*, TV), <Ja>パン</Ja> (*pan*, bread).
- **Foreign names and places**: <Ja>スイス</Ja> (*Suisu*, Switzerland), <Ja>ドイツ</Ja> (*Doitsu*, Germany), <Ja>ジャスミン</Ja> (*Jasumin*, Jasmin).
- **Emphasis**, sound effects and scientific names, a bit like italics in English.

In Katakana, a long vowel is usually written with a dash, <Ja>ー</Ja>: <Ja>コーヒー</Ja> is *kō-hī*.

<Callout type="note" title="Similar pairs">
  Some Katakana look alike and are easy to confuse at first: <Ja>シ</Ja> (*shi*) and <Ja>ツ</Ja> (*tsu*), or <Ja>ソ</Ja> (*so*) and <Ja>ン</Ja> (*n*). The difference is the long stroke: in <Ja>シ</Ja> and <Ja>ン</Ja> it is written upwards from the bottom left, while in <Ja>ツ</Ja> and <Ja>ソ</Ja> it comes down from the top.
</Callout>

## Kanji

**Kanji** are characters that were adopted from Chinese. Unlike kana, each Kanji carries a **meaning**, and usually has one or more readings. For example, <Ja>山</Ja> means "mountain" and <Ja>水</Ja> means "water".

There are thousands of Kanji. As a reference point, the official list of Kanji for general use, the **Jōyō Kanji** list, was last revised in 2010 and contains 2,136 characters [@joyo2010]. Learners start with a few dozen common ones and build up gradually.

Why keep Kanji when kana could write everything? One reason is that Japanese has many words that sound the same. Kanji make the meaning clear at a glance, and because Japanese doesn't use spaces between words, the switch between Kanji and kana also helps you see where words begin and end.

When a Kanji might be unknown to the reader, small Hiragana called **furigana** can be printed above it to show the reading: <Ja><ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby></Ja>.

## On’yomi and Kun’yomi

Most Kanji have two kinds of readings:

- **On’yomi** (<Ja>音読み</Ja>, "sound reading") comes from the Chinese pronunciation the character had when it was borrowed. It is often used when Kanji are combined into compound words.
- **Kun’yomi** (<Ja>訓読み</Ja>, "meaning reading") is the native Japanese word for the same meaning. It is often used when a Kanji stands alone or with Hiragana endings.

| Kanji | Meaning | Kun’yomi | On’yomi | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <Ja>山</Ja> | mountain | *yama* | *san* | <Ja>富士山</Ja> *Fujisan* (Mount Fuji) |
| <Ja>人</Ja> | person | *hito* | *jin*, *nin* | <Ja>日本人</Ja> *nihonjin* (Japanese person) |
| <Ja>水</Ja> | water | *mizu* | *sui* | <Ja>水曜日</Ja> *suiyōbi* (Wednesday) |
| <Ja>日</Ja> | sun, day | *hi* | *nichi*, *jitsu* | <Ja>日曜日</Ja> *nichiyōbi* (Sunday) |

This is a rule of thumb, not a strict law, and there are many exceptions. A good dictionary shows all readings of a Kanji and which one is used in a word [@jisho].

## Romaji

**Romaji** (<Ja>ローマ字</Ja>) means writing Japanese with the Latin alphabet, as in this guide's transcriptions: *nihongo*, *sushi*, *arigatō*. The most common system for learners is the **Hepburn** system, which spells sounds the way English speakers would expect, for example *shi* and *tsu*. Long vowels are often shown with a macron: *ō*, *ū*.

Romaji is useful at the very start and for typing Japanese on a computer, where you type *nihongo* and convert it to <Ja>日本語</Ja>. But Japanese people don't read Romaji in everyday life, so it's worth moving to kana as early as possible.

## How the three systems work together

Here is a typical sentence:

<p lang="ja" class="example-sentence">私は日本語を勉強しています。</p>

*Watashi wa nihongo o benkyō shite imasu.* – "I am studying Japanese."

| Part | Script | Reading | Role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <Ja>私</Ja> | Kanji | *watashi* | I |
| <Ja>は</Ja> | Hiragana | *wa* | topic particle |
| <Ja>日本語</Ja> | Kanji | *nihongo* | Japanese (language) |
| <Ja>を</Ja> | Hiragana | *o* | object particle |
| <Ja>勉強</Ja> | Kanji | *benkyō* | study |
| <Ja>しています</Ja> | Hiragana | *shite imasu* | "am doing" |

Notice that the particle <Ja>は</Ja> is pronounced *wa* (not *ha*) and <Ja>を</Ja> is pronounced *o* when they're used as particles.

A few more examples:

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Ja>私は学生です。</Ja> | Watashi wa gakusei desu. | I am a student. |
| <Ja>コーヒーを飲みます。</Ja> | Kōhī o nomimasu. | I drink coffee. |
| <Ja>スイスから来ました。</Ja> | Suisu kara kimashita. | I came from Switzerland. |
| <Ja>ありがとうございます。</Ja> | Arigatō gozaimasu. | Thank you very much. |

In <Ja>コーヒーを飲みます。</Ja> you can see all three systems at once: Katakana for the loanword, Hiragana for the particle and the verb ending, and Kanji for the verb stem.

## Conclusion

Japanese uses three writing systems because each one does something different. Hiragana handles grammar and native words, Katakana marks loanwords and foreign names, and Kanji carry meaning and make texts easier to read at a glance. A practical learning path is to master Hiragana first, then Katakana, and then add Kanji step by step, always learning them as part of real words.
