We expect the sun to rise tomorrow because it always has. Hume asked what, exactly, entitles us to that expectation [@hume1748].

## Two kinds of reasoning

Hume divides the objects of reasoning into two kinds:

- **Relations of ideas** – truths of mathematics and logic, knowable by thought alone. Denying them leads to contradiction.
- **Matters of fact** – claims about the world, such as “fire is hot”. Their opposite is always conceivable.

All reasoning about matters of fact beyond our present experience rests on **cause and effect**, and our knowledge of causes comes from experience, not from reason alone.

## The argument as a dilemma

1. Inferences from the observed to the unobserved assume that the future will resemble the past.[^uniformity]
2. This assumption could only be justified by demonstrative reasoning or by experience.
3. It cannot be demonstrated: a change in the course of nature is conceivable without contradiction.
4. It cannot be justified by experience without arguing in a circle – that would itself be an inductive inference.
5. So the assumption, and every inductive inference built on it, has no rational justification.

> Hume’s conclusion is not that we should stop reasoning inductively – we cannot – but that the habit is grounded in custom rather than in reason.

## Classic responses

### Pragmatic vindication

If any method of predicting the future works, induction will work too. It may not be *justified*, but it is the best bet we have.

### Falsificationism

Karl Popper argued that science never needed induction: it proposes bold conjectures and tries to refute them.

### Dissolving the problem

Some philosophers hold that asking for a justification of induction as a whole is confused, because induction is part of what “being reasonable” means.
