A black hole is a place in space where gravity is so strong that nothing can escape once it has crossed a certain boundary. Not even light. This boundary is called the **event horizon**.

Because no light comes out, we can't see a black hole directly. But we can see what happens around it. Stars move in unusual ways, gas heats up and glows as it falls inwards, and merging black holes send out tiny ripples in space and time, known as gravitational waves. Very sharp radio images can even show the area right next to a black hole [@nasa-bh].

One black hole is especially close to home: **Sagittarius A\*** (Sgr A\* for short), the giant black hole at the centre of our own galaxy, the Milky Way. Before we look at it more closely, we first need to understand what a black hole actually is, and how Albert Einstein changed the way we think about gravity.

## What is a black hole?

Black holes are often imagined as cosmic vacuum cleaners that suck in everything around them. That isn't quite right. From far away, a black hole pulls on things just like any other object with the same mass. Planets, stars and gas clouds can orbit a black hole for a very long time without ever falling in.

A thought experiment helps here. Imagine the Sun were suddenly replaced by a black hole with exactly the same mass. The Earth would carry on orbiting it in almost the same way as before. Of course, without sunlight life on Earth would be in serious trouble, but the orbit itself would barely change. So what makes a black hole special is not that its gravity is stronger at every distance. It is that a huge amount of mass is squeezed into a tiny space. Only very close to it does gravity become so extreme that nothing can escape any more.

### What a black hole is made of

**Event horizon.** The event horizon is the "point of no return". Anything that crosses it, including light, can never send a signal back out to the rest of the universe. It is important to know that the event horizon is not a solid surface you could stand on. It is simply a boundary in space. At least for a large black hole, someone falling through it would not notice anything special at that exact moment (more on this under tidal forces below).

**Singularity.** If we follow Einstein's equations all the way to the centre of an idealised black hole, they predict a point where density and curvature become infinitely large. This is called a **singularity**. Most physicists see it as a sign that the theory has reached its limits rather than a description of something real. Nobody knows what really happens there. A future theory that combines gravity with quantum physics might change the picture completely. Singularities are therefore a prediction of the theory, not something that has ever been observed.

**Accretion disc.** Some black holes are surrounded by gas that slowly spirals inwards, a bit like water swirling around a drain. The gas rubs against itself, heats up to enormous temperatures and glows brightly. This glowing ring is called an **accretion disc**. Not every black hole has one, though: a black hole with no gas nearby can be almost completely invisible.

**Photon sphere and shadow.** Near a black hole, gravity bends light so strongly that light can even travel in a circle around it. In the simplest model, a non-rotating black hole, this happens at one particular distance called the **photon sphere**. These circular paths are unstable, so light either escapes or falls in after a while. Because so much light is bent or captured, a black hole surrounded by glowing gas appears as a dark patch, its **shadow**. The shadow looks bigger than the event horizon itself, roughly twice its size [@nasa-anatomy].

## How do black holes form?

The best-understood way for a black hole to form starts with a very massive star. For most of its life, a star is in a kind of balance. Gravity tries to pull all its material inwards, while the heat and pressure from nuclear fusion in its core push outwards.

When a massive star runs out of fuel, this balance breaks. The core can no longer hold itself up and collapses under its own weight within a fraction of a second. What is left depends on the mass of the core. It can become a **neutron star**, an extremely dense object only about the size of a city, or, if there is enough mass, a **black hole**. The collapse is often linked to a huge explosion called a **supernova**, but not every black hole is born in a bright explosion.

Astronomers sort black holes into groups by their mass:

- **Stellar-mass black holes** have a few to a few dozen times the mass of the Sun. They form from collapsing stars.
- **Intermediate-mass black holes** would lie somewhere in between. There are some promising candidates, but their existence is still being studied.
- **Supermassive black holes** contain millions or even billions of times the mass of the Sun. They sit at the centres of most large galaxies, including our own.

How the first supermassive black holes grew so big so early in the history of the universe is still an open question. They may have started as smaller "seeds" and then grown by swallowing gas and merging with other black holes. Researchers are still working out which of these processes mattered most.

## Einstein's general theory of relativity

For more than 200 years, Isaac Newton's law of gravity explained almost everything: falling apples, the Moon's orbit and the paths of the planets. In 1915 Albert Einstein published a new theory, **general relativity**, which looks at gravity in a completely different way.

In Einstein's theory, gravity is not really a force that pulls on objects. Instead, mass and energy bend **spacetime**, which is space and time combined into one. Objects then simply follow the straightest possible path through this curved spacetime. Such a path is called a **geodesic**. The Earth, for example, isn't being pulled sideways by the Sun all the time; it follows a path through the curved spacetime around the Sun.

You may have seen the popular picture of a heavy ball lying on a stretched rubber sheet. The ball makes a dip, and smaller marbles roll towards it. This picture is useful, but it is only an analogy. Real spacetime has three dimensions of space plus time, and a flat sheet cannot show everything about it. In particular, the bending of time plays an important role, as we will see later.

Einstein described this idea with his field equations:

$$
G_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu\nu}.
$$

It looks complicated, but the basic idea is simple. The left side describes the **shape of spacetime**: $G_{\mu\nu}$ stands for its curvature, and $g_{\mu\nu}$ (the metric) says how distances and times are measured. The right side describes **what is in spacetime**: $T_{\mu\nu}$ stands for matter and energy. $G$ is the gravitational constant and $c$ the speed of light. Put simply: matter and energy determine how spacetime curves, and that curvature determines how things move. $\Lambda$, the cosmological constant, matters only on the scale of the whole universe and plays no real role near a single black hole.

### The Schwarzschild radius

Only a few months after Einstein published his theory, the astronomer Karl Schwarzschild found an exact solution to these equations. It was published in 1916 and describes the space around a perfectly round mass that does not rotate. This solution contains a special distance, the radius of the event horizon:

$$
r_s=\frac{2GM}{c^2},
$$

where $M$ is the mass. This distance is called the **Schwarzschild radius**. The formula tells us how small an object of a certain mass would have to be squeezed for it to become a black hole. The radius grows in direct proportion to the mass: twice the mass means twice the radius.

The numbers are surprising. For the Sun, the Schwarzschild radius is only about **2.95 km**. For the Earth, it is less than one centimetre, about 9 mm. This doesn't mean that the Sun or the Earth are black holes. They are far larger than these sizes, so they don't have an event horizon. The formula simply shows how extreme the squeezing would have to be.

One more detail: real black holes usually spin. For rotating black holes, a different solution, the **Kerr solution**, gives a more accurate description. The Schwarzschild model is the simplest version, which is why it is used so often to explain the basic ideas.

## What happens to space and time near a black hole?

### Gravitational time dilation

One of the most surprising predictions of general relativity is that gravity affects time. A clock in a strong gravitational field ticks more slowly than a clock far away. This effect is called **gravitational time dilation**. It is not only a theory: it is so measurable that satellite navigation systems such as GPS have to correct for it.

Near a black hole, the effect becomes extreme. For a clock that stays still at a distance $r$ from a non-rotating black hole, compared with a clock very far away, the following relation applies:

$$
d\tau=dt\sqrt{1-\frac{2GM}{rc^2}}=dt\sqrt{1-\frac{r_s}{r}}.
$$

Here $d\tau$ is the time that passes on the clock near the black hole, $dt$ is the time that passes for a distant observer, and $r$ is the distance from the centre. The square root is always smaller than 1, so less time passes near the black hole.

A few examples make this clearer:

- At ten times the Schwarzschild radius, the clock runs at about 95 % of the speed of the distant clock.
- At twice the Schwarzschild radius ($r = 2r_s$), it runs at only about 71 %.
- Just outside the horizon, at $r = 1.1\,r_s$, it runs at only about 30 %.

The closer the clock gets to the horizon, the slower it ticks compared with the distant clock.

This formula only works under certain conditions: the black hole must not rotate, the clock must stay in place, and it must be outside the horizon. The formula also does **not** mean that time stops for someone who falls into a black hole. For them, their own clock keeps ticking normally. Only the comparison with a distant clock shows the difference.

### Gravitational lensing

Light always travels along the straightest possible path. In curved spacetime, however, even the straightest path is bent. This is called **gravitational lensing**, because a heavy object bends light a bit like a glass lens does.

Near a black hole, this bending becomes extreme. Light from gas or stars behind the black hole can be bent around it, so they appear distorted, stretched or even more than once. Some light rays go partly around the black hole before they escape; others fall in and disappear. That is why realistic images of black holes show bright, warped rings rather than a simple dark ball [@nasa-anatomy].

### Tidal forces

Gravity gets weaker the further you are from a mass. So if an object is close to a black hole, the end nearer the black hole is pulled harder than the end further away. This difference stretches the object. Such differences in gravity are called **tidal forces**. The same effect, on a much smaller scale, is what causes the tides on Earth: the Moon pulls a little more strongly on the side of the Earth facing it.

How strong these forces are depends on the mass of the black hole and on the distance from it. Surprisingly, they are weaker at the horizon of a large black hole than at the horizon of a small one. Near a stellar-mass black hole, an astronaut would be stretched apart long before reaching the event horizon. At the horizon of a supermassive black hole, on the other hand, the tidal forces would be far too weak to notice at first.

## Sagittarius A\*: the black hole in our galaxy

At the centre of our galaxy, about **27,000 light-years** from Earth, there is a compact source of radio waves called **Sagittarius A\***. It is associated with a supermassive black hole with roughly **four million times the mass of the Sun** [@eso2022]. Its Schwarzschild radius is about 12 million kilometres, which is tiny compared with the size of the galaxy around it.

### How did astronomers find it?

A black hole can't be seen directly, so astronomers looked at its effects instead. For decades, research teams followed the motion of individual stars near the centre of the Milky Way.

One star in particular, **S2** (also called S0-2), has become famous. It orbits the centre of the galaxy about once every **16 years**. At its closest point, it moves at almost three per cent of the speed of light [@eso2020]. At that speed, it would cover the distance from the Earth to the Moon in less than a minute.

To make a star move so fast in such a tight orbit, an enormous mass must be packed into a very small space. The stellar orbits show that about four million solar masses are hidden in a region so small that no known type of object other than a black hole fits the measurements. This makes the orbits strong evidence for a supermassive black hole. Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez shared half of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery of a supermassive compact object at the centre of our galaxy [@nobel2020].

The orbit of S2 has also been used to test Einstein's theory. When the star passes closest to the black hole, its light is stretched to longer wavelengths by the strong gravity. This effect is called **gravitational redshift**. Astronomers have also measured a **Schwarzschild precession** of the orbit. This means that the orbit is not a perfectly closed ellipse: with each lap, it turns a little, so that over many orbits its path traces out a rosette shape. Both effects match Einstein's predictions [@eso2020].

### The 2022 Event Horizon Telescope image

On **12 May 2022**, the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) Collaboration presented the first image of Sagittarius A\*. Taking this picture was a huge challenge. Seen from Earth, the black hole appears about as big in the sky as a doughnut on the Moon would [@eso2022].

No single telescope can see such small details. So in **2017**, radio observatories around the world observed Sagittarius A\* at the same time. Their data were later combined using a technique called **very-long-baseline interferometry**. This creates a virtual telescope as large as the Earth itself [@eso2022; @eht2022].

The image shows a **bright ring of radio light around a darker centre**. It is important to understand what this image does and doesn't show. It is not a photo of the inside of a black hole, and it doesn't show the event horizon directly. The bright ring is light from hot gas around the black hole. The dark centre is the **shadow** of the black hole, created because the strong gravity bends and captures light. The size of the ring matches the predictions for a black hole with the mass that had already been calculated from the stellar orbits [@eht2022]. Two completely different methods therefore lead to the same result.

Sagittarius A\* was particularly difficult to photograph. The gas around it circles the black hole in just a few minutes, so the image changes constantly, a bit like trying to take a sharp photo of a running child. On top of that, the radio waves are scattered by material between Earth and the centre of the galaxy. Creating the final image therefore required a lot of careful analysis and many independent checks [@eht2022].

<Callout type="note" title="Observation and model">
  What has been **observed** at Sagittarius A\* are the orbits of stars, the gravitational redshift and precession of S2, and a ring of radio emission around a shadow. That these observations come from a black hole with an event horizon is the **interpretation** that fits them best within general relativity. Singularities, Hawking radiation and the inner structure of black holes are **theoretical** topics; none of them has been observed at Sagittarius A\*.
</Callout>

## Why these observations matter

When general relativity was first published, many of its predictions seemed almost unbelievable: that light could be bent by gravity, that clocks could run at different speeds, and that spacetime could be curved so strongly that nothing escapes. Today, scientists can test these ideas in several independent ways: through the orbits of stars around Sagittarius A\*, through gravitational waves from merging black holes, and through images like the one from the EHT.

So far, Einstein's theory has passed all these tests. But important questions remain. General relativity describes gravity and the universe on large scales, while quantum physics describes the world of the tiniest particles. Both theories work extremely well in their own areas, but nobody yet knows how to combine them. Black holes are exactly where this problem becomes impossible to ignore, because there huge masses and tiny distances come together. Ideas such as Hawking radiation and the question of what happens to information that falls into a black hole are important topics in theoretical physics. However, they have not been observed at Sagittarius A\*.

## Conclusion

Black holes show that gravity is much more than a force that pulls objects together. According to Einstein, it is part of the shape of spacetime itself. The event horizon, the slowing of time and the bending of light all follow from this idea. Sagittarius A\* turns these ideas into something we can actually test, through the orbits of nearby stars and through images of the glowing gas close to its horizon.

That is what makes black holes so fascinating. They are some of the most extreme objects in the universe, and at the same time natural laboratories. They show where our understanding of physics already works remarkably well, and where it still has gaps.
