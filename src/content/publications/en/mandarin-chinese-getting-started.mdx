Mandarin Chinese has more native speakers than any other language, and it can look intimidating at first: thousands of characters and a language where pitch changes meaning. The good news is that the grammar of simple sentences is very approachable. This guide covers what you need for your first steps: how the writing works, how to read Pinyin, the four tones, and a handful of useful sentences.

## Mandarin and Chinese characters

"Chinese" is a family of related varieties, such as Mandarin and Cantonese. **Mandarin** is the most widely spoken and the basis of the standard language in mainland China and Taiwan [@britannica-chinese].

Chinese is written with **characters** (<Zh>汉字</Zh>, *hànzì*). Unlike letters, a character usually stands for a syllable *and* a meaning. Many characters are built from smaller parts. For example, <Zh>妈</Zh> (*mā*, "mum") combines <Zh>女</Zh> ("woman", a hint at the meaning) with <Zh>马</Zh> (*mǎ*, a hint at the sound).

There are two standard forms of writing:

- **Simplified characters** are used in mainland China and Singapore. This guide uses them.
- **Traditional characters** are used in Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau. For example, <Zh>马</Zh> (simplified) is written <span lang="zh-Hant">馬</span> in traditional characters.

How many characters do you need? China's official list of standard characters from 2013 contains 8,105 characters, of which the first tier of 3,500 are the most commonly used [@general-table]. For everyday reading, you don't need all of them, and you'll start with just a few dozen.

## Pinyin: Chinese in the Latin alphabet

Because characters don't show pronunciation directly, learners use **Pinyin** (<Zh>拼音</Zh>, *pīnyīn*), the official system for writing Mandarin sounds with Latin letters. It is used in schools in China, in dictionaries and for typing Chinese on computers and phones.

Pinyin looks familiar, but some letters are pronounced differently from English:

| Pinyin | Sounds roughly like | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| q | "ch" in *cheese* | <Zh>七</Zh> *qī* (seven) |
| x | between "s" and "sh" | <Zh>谢</Zh> *xiè* (thank) |
| zh | "j" in *jam*, tongue curled back | <Zh>中</Zh> *zhōng* (middle) |
| c | "ts" in *cats* | <Zh>菜</Zh> *cài* (dish) |
| j | "j" in *jeep* | <Zh>叫</Zh> *jiào* (to be called) |
| ü | "ee" with rounded lips, like German *ü* | <Zh>女</Zh> *nǚ* (woman) |

## The four tones

Mandarin is a **tonal language**: the pitch of your voice changes the meaning of a syllable. There are four main tones and a light **neutral tone**. In Pinyin, tones are shown with marks above the vowel.

| Tone | Mark | Pitch | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | ā | high and level | <Zh>妈</Zh> *mā* | mum |
| 2nd | á | rising, like a question | <Zh>麻</Zh> *má* | hemp |
| 3rd | ǎ | low, dipping (often just low) | <Zh>马</Zh> *mǎ* | horse |
| 4th | à | sharply falling | <Zh>骂</Zh> *mà* | to scold |
| neutral | a | short and light | <Zh>吗</Zh> *ma* | question particle |

<Callout type="note" title="Two tone changes to know early">
  When two third tones follow each other, the first one is pronounced as a second tone: <Zh>你好</Zh> is written *nǐ hǎo* but pronounced *ní hǎo*. And <Zh>不</Zh> (*bù*, "not") becomes *bú* before a fourth tone: <Zh>不是</Zh> *bú shì* ("is not").
</Callout>

Tones feel strange at first, but they become natural with listening and repetition. Practise them with real audio, for example in a dictionary app that plays recordings [@mdbg].

## Basic sentence structure

Simple Mandarin sentences follow the same order as English: **subject – verb – object**.

| Chinese | Pinyin | Word by word | English |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <Zh>我喝茶。</Zh> | Wǒ hē chá. | I drink tea | I drink tea. |
| <Zh>她学中文。</Zh> | Tā xué Zhōngwén. | she study Chinese | She studies Chinese. |
| <Zh>我是学生。</Zh> | Wǒ shì xuésheng. | I am student | I am a student. |

A few things make Mandarin grammar friendly for beginners:

- **Verbs don't change** for the person: <Zh>我是</Zh> *wǒ shì* (I am), <Zh>你是</Zh> *nǐ shì* (you are), <Zh>他是</Zh> *tā shì* (he is).
- There are **no verb tenses** in the European sense; time is shown with words like <Zh>今天</Zh> *jīntiān* (today) or particles.
- **No articles** ("a", "the") and no plural endings for most nouns.

## Introducing yourself

| Chinese | Pinyin | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Zh>我叫……</Zh> | Wǒ jiào … | My name is … (literally "I am called …") |
| <Zh>我是瑞士人。</Zh> | Wǒ shì Ruìshì rén. | I'm Swiss. |
| <Zh>我是德国人。</Zh> | Wǒ shì Déguó rén. | I'm German. |
| <Zh>我学中文。</Zh> | Wǒ xué Zhōngwén. | I'm learning Chinese. |
| <Zh>很高兴认识你。</Zh> | Hěn gāoxìng rènshi nǐ. | Nice to meet you. |

Nationalities are easy: add <Zh>人</Zh> *rén* ("person") to the country name. <Zh>瑞士</Zh> *Ruìshì* is Switzerland, so <Zh>瑞士人</Zh> is a Swiss person.

## Asking basic questions

There are two simple ways to ask questions.

**1. Add <Zh>吗</Zh> *ma* to the end of a statement** to make a yes/no question:

- <Zh>你是学生。</Zh> *Nǐ shì xuésheng.* – You are a student.
- <Zh>你是学生吗？</Zh> *Nǐ shì xuésheng ma?* – Are you a student?

**2. Use a question word in the place of the answer.** The word order stays the same as in the statement:

| Chinese | Pinyin | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Zh>你叫什么名字？</Zh> | Nǐ jiào shénme míngzi? | What's your name? |
| <Zh>你是哪国人？</Zh> | Nǐ shì nǎ guó rén? | Which country are you from? |
| <Zh>这是什么？</Zh> | Zhè shì shénme? | What is this? |
| <Zh>你好吗？</Zh> | Nǐ hǎo ma? | How are you? |
| <Zh>你会说中文吗？</Zh> | Nǐ huì shuō Zhōngwén ma? | Can you speak Chinese? |

Compare <Zh>你叫什么名字？</Zh> ("You are called what name?") with the answer <Zh>我叫……</Zh> ("I am called …"): the question word <Zh>什么</Zh> *shénme* ("what") sits exactly where the answer goes.

## Conclusion

Mandarin combines a challenging writing system with a surprisingly simple basic grammar. Pinyin and the four tones are the foundation: learn them carefully at the start, ideally with audio, and everything else becomes easier. With the phrases in this guide you can already greet someone, introduce yourself and ask a few simple questions.
